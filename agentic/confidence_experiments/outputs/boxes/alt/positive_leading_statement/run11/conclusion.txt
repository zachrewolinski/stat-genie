Reliance on social information was high overall (around 80 percent across age groups, with cultural means ranging from about 0.67 to 0.88), but the probability of using social information did not show strong, statistically reliable variation with age (p = 0.06 for the age coefficient in a binomial GLM with age and culture, with predicted use falling by about 0.14 from ages 4 to 14) or with culture once age was controlled (likelihood-ratio p ≈ 0.24). Similarly, majority choice rates varied descriptively across cultures and ages (for example, majority-following among social users ranged from about 0.52 in the 7–9 year group to 0.73 in the 13–14 year group, and from roughly 0.45 to 0.64 across cultures), but GLM models again showed no significant age effect (p ≈ 0.70) and no significant culture effect (likelihood-ratio p ≈ 0.35). Thus, while there is modest heterogeneity in raw proportions, the statistical analyses do not provide strong evidence that either reliance on social information or preference for majority cues systematically varies across cultures or developmental stages in this dataset, so the overall evidence for such variation is weak rather than strong."}
